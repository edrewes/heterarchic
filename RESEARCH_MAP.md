# Agora Research Map

**Initial map · 2026-10-28**

**Initial streams now include:** comparative and historical human governance; institutional design and collective action; AI and human–AI governance; delegation and agentic work; technology, institutional capacity and flourishing; and temporary or episodic self-governance, including protest movements, occupations, strikes, affinity groups and mutual-aid settings. The last stream is explicitly cross-scale and time-bounded: it covers governance practices as they arise during collective action, not only durable institutions.

## A. Human governance across time and cultures

**Questions:** What varied arrangements have people used to make rules, allocate authority, manage common resources, resolve disputes, represent groups, and constrain rulers? How did these work in practice and for whom? What are the limits of surviving records and comparative categories?

**Coverage:** kinship and customary institutions; councils and assemblies; city-states and empires; religious and legal orders; republics, monarchies, democracies, and federations; bureaucracies; labor and cooperative governance; commons and polycentric arrangements; colonial and postcolonial orders; contemporary community, platform, and transnational governance. Include bounded, episodic, and temporary governance at multiple scales—including protest movements, occupations, strikes, mutual-aid efforts, affinity groups, and other collective-action settings—alongside durable institutions.

**Guardrail:** Do not use a list of regime labels as a history. Include primary sources, context, affected people's accounts where available, and critical historiography. Avoid treating any single dataset or canon as a complete map of human experience.

## B. Bounded and episodic self-governance

**Questions:** How do people create, exercise, and end collective rules and delegated authority in time-bounded settings such as protest movements, occupations, strikes, mutual-aid responses, and affinity groups? Which arrangements govern participation, deliberation, action, care, security, resources, communication, representation, and internal conflict? How do practices change under urgency, repression, growth, or loss of shared space? What persists after the episode ends, and what does not?

**Coverage:** Compare particular practices (for example, assemblies, consensus or modified consensus, facilitation and stack, caucuses, working groups, spokes councils, strike and occupation committees, rotating or recallable delegates, supply and care infrastructures, and tactical coordination) across distinct settings. Treat participant accounts and rules as evidence of intended design; separately investigate observed practice, who had voice or power, outcomes, failure, and context. Do not assume that a protest’s self-governance was inclusive, representative, durable, or shared by all participants.

**Initial case leads:** Occupy Wall Street / Zuccotti Park; France’s May–June 1968 student and workplace occupations; Hong Kong’s 2014 Umbrella Movement; anarchist collectives and federations (with Spain 1936 as one historically distinct lead); and comparative protest-camp research. These are research leads, not yet completed or equivalent case studies.

**Analytical lens:** Map governance together with the material practices needed to sustain collective action: decision-making, action, communication, care and daily maintenance. A comparative framework from protest-camp scholarship groups these as governance, action, media/communication, and re-creation infrastructures; treat it as an organizing heuristic, not a universal typology (Frenzel, Feigenbaum, and McCurdy 2014). Examine how movement repertoire and infrastructures emerge, travel, and change across place and time (McCurdy, Feigenbaum, and Frenzel 2016).

## C. Institutional design and collective action

**Questions:** Which mechanisms support cooperation, accountability, learning, representation, conflict resolution, and adaptation under different conditions? How do power and incentives alter formal rules? How are these effects evaluated?

## D. AI and human–AI governance

**Questions:** Who is accountable across model providers, deployers, procurers, operators, and affected people? How can participation, rights, oversight, incident response, audit, contestability, and remedy be made effective? Which controls work at the actual integration boundary?

**Topics:** public-sector AI; organizational accountability; standards and risk management; data governance; human oversight; safety/security; labor impacts; international coordination; assurance and evaluation; institutional capacity.

## E. Delegation, handoffs, and agentic work

**Questions:** How should a human delegate bounded work to agents and automations? What should an authorization specify—principal, task, scope, permitted tools/actions, limits, expiry, evidence, escalation, revocation, subdelegation, and responsibility? How do we test enforcement and actual revocation, including in-flight actions?

**Initial posture:** deterministic, least-privilege controls and clear approvals first; model-based authority changes are research hypotheses, not default controls.

## F. Technology, institutional capacity, and flourishing

**Questions:** When do digital systems and LLMs improve access, coordination, learning, deliberation, public services, or organizational capacity? Who benefits, who bears costs, what gets measured, and what becomes harder to contest?

**Measures:** context-specific dashboards that can include material outcomes, health, agency/capabilities, distribution, social connection, participation, trust, sustainability, quality and timeliness, and burdens. Avoid a single proxy treated as the whole of flourishing.

## G. Research infrastructure and publication

Develop reusable source registries, comparative case records, transparent synthesis, evaluation protocols, and accessible outputs. Preserve provenance and versioning; do not scrape or republish restricted databases or copyrighted source collections without permission.

## Initial outputs

1. A comparative map of governance mechanisms, traditions, and evidence gaps, spanning both durable institutions and bounded, episodic settings of self-governance.
2. A human–AI delegation and handoff design brief with enforceability tests.
3. A framework for evaluating technology-enabled governance against human-centered outcomes and explicit baselines.

Each output must earn its claims under `RESEARCH_PROTOCOL.md` and the publication gate. No target date implies completion or validation.
