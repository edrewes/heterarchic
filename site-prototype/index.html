01 / The question

Not a museum of failures. Not a fantasy of perfect systems.

After all this time, what have we learned—and how can we use it to do better?

Public talk about governance often collapses into cynicism or confident simplifications: people are selfish; democracy never works; one tradition had the answer; technology will fix it. The historical record is more interesting—and more useful—than any of those shortcuts.

This project asks what different arrangements actually did, for whom, under what conditions, and at what cost. Then it asks what those lessons let us imagine and test now: institutions that are more capable, fair, answerable, and open to revision.

Our stance: curiosity without romanticism. Evidence without pretending it speaks for itself. Creativity without confusing a proposal with a proven result.

03 / Provisional questions to test

Lessons are questions you can test.

These are working hypotheses for inquiry—not settled findings from a completed comparative review. History offers mechanisms, trade-offs, failures, and questions to investigate.

Rules do not tell the whole story.

Compare formal design with actual practice, informal influence, and who could participate. Intended inclusion is not the same as experienced voice.

Look for rules + observed practice + affected people's accounts

Context changes what a mechanism can do.

Assemblies, councils, delegation, and consensus travel differently across scale, urgency, law, resources, and power. Similar labels do not make equivalent institutions.

Compare conditions before claiming transferability

Success and failure can coexist.

A process may coordinate action while struggling with legitimacy, time, representation, care, or conflict. Ask: successful at what, for whom, and over what horizon?

Name outcomes, trade-offs, and uncertainty

Better is something to design—and evaluate.

Use historical insight to generate proposals, then make the mechanism explicit: what should change, for whom, and what evidence would show the design did not work?

Proposal ≠ evidence; test against a stated baseline
04 / The method

Show the work.

This prototype uses a few seed sources already listed in the project’s source atlas. It does not claim to be a complete history or a settled synthesis. Every future case should make its evidence, interpretation, and gaps visible.

For visuals: this first build uses original, abstract diagrams—not historical imagery. Public-domain images or video can be added only when the specific asset’s provenance and rights are verified.

01

Describe: who made rules, allocated authority, resolved conflict, and revised decisions?

02

Source: pair primary records with scholarship, criticism, and affected-party perspectives where available.

03

Separate: mark documented facts, empirical findings, interpretations, values, and proposals distinctly.

04

Compare carefully: preserve context; surface counterevidence, missing voices, and limits of the record.

05

Imagine and test: turn a lesson into a design hypothesis, name what could falsify it, and keep corrections visible.
